Section 9Calibration and cost: turning a score into a decision

A probability is a claim about the world: "18% of clients who look like this will default". That claim can be checked, and it must be, because the cost analysis multiplies it by a dollar figure. This section derives calibration, derives the cost matrix from credit-risk parameters rather than inventing it, derives Elkan's analytic threshold, and shows that the empirical and analytic optima agree — which is simultaneously a cost result and a calibration proof.

9.1 What calibration means, precisely

The definition

A model is calibrated if, for every value q, among all clients assigned probability q the observed default rate is q:

P( Y = 1 | p̂(X) = q ) = q   for all q(17)

Two things follow, and they are the reason both discrimination and calibration must be reported.

Calibration is not accuracy. A model that outputs the base rate for every single client is perfectly calibrated and completely useless — its ROC-AUC is 0.5. Calibration constrains the scale of the predictions; discrimination constrains their ordering. They are orthogonal, and a usable model needs both.

Discrimination is not calibration. §4.6 showed arms A and C with indistinguishable ROC-AUC and a 0.045 Brier gap. ROC-AUC literally cannot see the difference, because it is invariant to monotone transforms and miscalibration is a monotone transform.

9.3 Why the raw probabilities are already nearly calibrated

Arm A's raw ECE is and its mean prediction against a base rate of — out of the box, with no calibration step. That is not luck, and being able to explain why is worth more than the number.

Log-loss is a strictly proper scoring rule

XGBoost with objective="binary:logistic" minimises log-loss. A strictly proper scoring rule is uniquely minimised by reporting the true conditional probability — so a model trained to minimise it is trained toward calibration, not merely toward ranking. Add that the initial margin is log(π/(1−π)) (the base rate) and that a heavily regularised ensemble is reluctant to produce extreme values, and near-calibration is the expected outcome.

Contrast three models that are not calibrated by construction: an SVM (hinge loss optimises a margin, not a probability — hence Platt's original 1999 paper); a random forest (an average of votes, which compresses toward 0.5 and is over-confident at the extremes); and any model fitted with class weights, per equation (16). Arm C is the third case, and it demonstrates exactly the predicted failure.

9.5 The cost matrix, derived rather than invented

Picking "FN costs 5× FP" because it sounds plausible is the weakest possible move here: it is the one assumption the entire decision layer rests on, and an invented number makes every downstream figure arbitrary. So both costs come from standard credit-risk quantities.

Both sides of the matrix, from first principles

A false negative is a default the lender funded. The loss is the exposure at default multiplied by the fraction not recovered:

CFN = LGD × EAD,   LGD ≈ 0.70(18)

0.70 is the standard retail-unsecured loss-given-default: revolving credit-card debt is uncollateralised, so recovery is partial collection on a defaulted balance.

A false positive is a creditworthy client refused. Nothing is lost outright — the lender forgoes the margin it would have earned on that exposure:

CFP = NIM × EAD,   NIM ≈ 0.12(19)

0.12 is a representative net interest margin on revolving credit-card lending.

The ratio is what the threshold depends on, and the exposure term cancels out of it:

r = CFN/CFP = LGD/NIM = 0.70/0.12 = 5.83 → 6 : 1(20)
Why EAD cancelling is the important part

Equation (20) means the optimal threshold does not depend on how large the exposures are — only on the ratio of the two rates. That is why the headline analysis can use a flat per-error cost and still be defensible, and why the whole r sweep below is meaningful: r is a single interpretable dial encoding a lender's risk appetite, and a reader who disagrees with LGD = 0.70 can read their own row off the sweep rather than discarding the analysis.

The NT$1.17 bn figure is motivation, never a result

The proposal opened with a market-scale figure (9.8 M cardholders, NT$1.17 bn of defaulted balances). It belongs in the Introduction as motivation and nowhere else. Multiplying a cost saving measured on clients up to a national portfolio would require assuming this sample is representative of the whole market, that the model transfers across issuers, and that decisions scale linearly — none of which is established. The project reports cost on the actual evaluated clients, in error units and in LIMIT_BAL-weighted units, and stops there.

9.6 Elkan's analytic threshold

Derivation — four lines, and worth memorising

For a client with default probability p, compare the two expected costs:

E[cost | reject] = (1 − p) · CFP      E[cost | accept] = p · CFN

Rejecting is worth it when the second exceeds the first:

p · CFN > (1 − p) · CFP  ⟺  p · r > 1 − p  ⟺  p > 1/(1 + r)

So the optimal decision threshold is purely a function of the cost ratio (Elkan, 2001):

p* = 1/(1 + r)(21)

9.7 The result that ties calibration and cost together

Here is the part worth putting in the body of the report. The project computes the threshold twice, by two methods that share no information:

  • Empirically — sweep 51 thresholds over out-of-fold training probabilities and take the cost-minimising one. Uses the data, uses the model, uses no formula.
  • Analytically — equation (21). Uses the cost ratio only; no data, no model.

If the probabilities carry the right scale and not merely the right order, the two must agree. Over the whole sweep the mean absolute deviation is on raw probabilities, falling to after isotonic calibration.

9.8 Instance-weighted costing

Flat costing assumes every client's exposure is identical, which is false: LIMIT_BAL spans NT$10,000 to NT$1,000,000. So the sweep is also run with per-client costs using LIMIT_BAL as an EAD proxy — total cost then sums over clients rather than being count × constant.

The effect is large and in a consistent direction. Under instance weighting the optimal threshold is at every cost ratio, because the objective is now dominated by a small number of high-limit clients and the ranking of whom to flag changes. Recall rises to .

Why both schemes are reported rather than just the better one

LIMIT_BAL is a proxy. The true exposure at default is the drawn balance at the moment of default, which is unobservable here — a client with a NT$500,000 limit carrying NT$3,000 of debt is not a NT$500,000 exposure. Using the credit limit systematically overstates exposure for low-utilisation clients, and §2 showed utilisation varies enormously.

So the flat scheme is the headline (defensible, no proxy assumption) and the instance scheme is reported alongside as a sensitivity. Reporting both, and naming the proxy's weakness, is stronger than picking whichever produces the more impressive saving — which would have been the instance scheme, and would have been the less defensible choice.

src/costs.py — the full decision layer in three functions
def cost_at_threshold(y, proba, tau, r=R_HEADLINE, ead=None):
    """Total cost of thresholding `proba` at `tau`. Flat unless `ead` is given."""
    pred = proba >= tau                       # never .predict()
    fn = (y == 1) & ~pred
    fp = (y == 0) & pred
    if ead is None:
        return float(r * fn.sum() + fp.sum())
    return float(r * ead[fn].sum() + ead[fp].sum())   # instance-weighted

def elkan_p_star(r=R_HEADLINE):
    """Elkan (2001) analytic optimum. No data, no model -- just the ratio."""
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + r)

def pick_threshold_oof(y_oof, proba_oof, r=R_HEADLINE, ead=None, grid=None):
    """Frozen scalar from out-of-fold TRAINING predictions. See section 5.5."""
    grid  = threshold_grid() if grid is None else np.asarray(grid)
    costs = np.array([cost_at_threshold(y_oof, proba_oof, t, r, ead) for t in grid])
    return float(grid[int(np.argmin(costs))]), {...}
Self-check: at r = 6 the chosen threshold gives 33% precision — two in three flagged clients would have repaid. Is that acceptable?

Yes, and that is the whole point of costing the errors rather than counting them. At r = 6 one missed default costs as much as six unnecessary refusals, so trading one recovered default for up to six false positives is profitable. The frozen operating point catches of defaulters at precision, and the total cost is against at τ = 0.5.

Low precision is not a defect here; it is the correct response to an asymmetric cost function. Reporting precision without the cost ratio invites exactly this objection, which is why the two always appear together. And if a reader rejects LGD = 0.70, the sweep lets them read the operating point for their own r: at r = 1 the model runs at precision instead.

Self-check: why sweep r at all, instead of just reporting r = 6?

Three reasons, in increasing importance.

Robustness. LGD and NIM are estimates. A reader who believes LGD = 0.45 or NIM = 0.20 needs to know whether the conclusion survives, and the sweep answers that without re-running anything.

It is what makes the Elkan check possible. A single r gives one (τ, p*) pair, and one point proves nothing — any formula can match one point. Six ratios trace a curve, and the empirical optima tracking 1/(1+r) across the entire range is what turns coincidence into evidence. The mean absolute deviation of is a statement about the curve, not a point.

It separates the model from the policy. The sweep makes explicit that the model supplies a probability and the business supplies r. That is the correct division of responsibility, and it is exactly what a regulator reviewing an automated credit decision would want to see documented.
